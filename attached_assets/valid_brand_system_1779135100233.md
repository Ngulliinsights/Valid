# VALID
## Brand Language System

*The space between knowing and responding. That is where this brand lives.*

---

**Method:** Four-persona strategic audit  
**Scope:** Color · Typography · Voice · Visual Language · Application  
**Versions:** Social (Real Talk) · Professional (Therapeutic Connections)  
**Governing rule:** Nothing survives unless all four voices approve it

---

## 01 — The Method: Four Voices, One Survivor

Mental health brand aesthetics collapse to the least-objectionable choice: teal, sage, rounded sans-serifs, stock photography of calm people. To escape that gravity, every direction in this system was run through four distinct expert lenses simultaneously. Only what survived all four voices became the system.

---

### Dr. Amara Osei
*Licensed Clinical Psychologist · 18 years practice · APA Ethics review experience*

**Rejected:** Teal-and-sage wellness palette. Rounded, friendly sans-serifs. "When I see the aesthetic of a meditation app, I assume the product is performing wellness, not building it. Clinicians can tell the difference immediately."

**Approved:** Warm amber as primary. Weighted humanist serif for scenario text. "This signals that something serious was read before something pretty was chosen. The type says the team understands earned authority."

> *"Clinicians won't recommend a product that looks like it belongs at a spa. The visual language is also a credibility claim."*

---

### Theo Bassi
*Lived Experience Advocate · Bipolar I · Mental health community, 40K+ · Policy contributor*

**Rejected:** Pastel accents. "Friendly" rounded type. Soft imagery. "The entire wellness palette was designed for the worried well, not the actually unwell. It optimizes for the caregiver's discomfort, not the patient's reality."

**Approved:** Cormorant Garamond + DM Sans. No pastels. High contrast. "This treats me like someone who has read. It doesn't assume I need the edges rounded off to handle the subject."

> *"Pastels comfort the person designing the product more than the person using it. I need to know you're taking this seriously."*

---

### Elspeth Vance
*Brand Strategist · ex-Wolff Olins · Social impact identity specialist · 20 years*

**Rejected:** Blues, greens, abstract human figures. Soft gradients. "That territory is so saturated you would need a billboard to get noticed. Hospital systems, therapy apps, crisis hotlines — all identical. Zero differentiation."

**Approved:** Amber-on-parchment. Cormorant Garamond as a mental health brand typeface. Card geometry as primary motif. "This is white space in the market. I can picture it next to a quality chess set. Premium without signaling luxury."

> *"The type choice alone occupies unclaimed territory. Every other mental health brand is running the same Poppins/Nunito play."*

---

### Prof. Jiro Wakabayashi
*Cognitive Neuroscientist · Visual attention under stress · Color and decision-making research*

**Rejected:** Cool blue-green for a skill-practice product. "Environmental color research is context-dependent. In clinical spaces, cool palettes reduce arousal. For a game asking people to approach difficult material — that is precisely wrong."

**Approved:** Warm amber primary. High-contrast Ground/Parchment. "Approach-motivation research supports warm tones for engagement tasks. High contrast reduces working memory load during emotionally demanding reading."

> *"Valid needs players leaning in, not relaxing away. Cool palettes activate the wrong motivational system for a practice context."*

## 02 — The Surviving Direction

The direction is not a compromise between four perspectives. It is the position that each persona, from their specific expertise, independently ratified. That convergence is not consensus — it is evidence.

> **Brand Position — Approved by all four voices**
>
> Valid is not a wellness brand. It is a precision skill-building system that happens to care deeply about the humans using it. The visual language must feel like a lamp in a crisis room: warm enough to stay, specific enough to trust.

### Valid Is Not

- A meditation or mindfulness app
- A mental health awareness campaign
- A clinical services or therapy brand
- A crisis platform or hotline
- Optimized for the worried well
- Soft because the subject matter is hard
- Performing care rather than building it

### Valid Is

- A precision skill-building game
- A practice environment before the real moment
- A bridge between clinical knowledge and everyday use
- Warm because warmth enables approach, not avoidance
- Serious because the stakes actually are
- Evidence-grounded in every aesthetic decision
- Built for the people in the room when someone they love is in crisis

## 03 — Color System

Six colors. All warm-biased. None chosen from the mental health brand defaults. Each name references a material, not a mood — because Valid is built on what things are, not on what you hope they will feel like.

| Name | Hex | Role |
|------|-----|------|
| **Ground** | `#1A1814` | Primary text. Everything rests on this. |
| **Parchment** | `#F2EDDF` | Primary background. Warm, not clinical white. |
| **Ember** | `#C4882A` | Brand primary. The moment of understanding. |
| **Resin** | `#6B3D2E` | Deep accent. Grounds without coldness. |
| **Drift** | `#9A9488` | Secondary text, labels, metadata. |
| **Tide** | `#3D6B65` | Professional version only. Reserved and distinct. |

### Color Rationale

**Osei → Ember as primary:** Amber signals earned warmth and attention — distinct from the performative calm of teal. Clinicians associate amber light with the moment things shift in a session. It does not evade the difficulty.

**Bassi → Ground + Parchment:** High contrast respects the reader. It does not assume they need the world softened. Parchment is warm without being a pastel — it carries weight rather than deflecting it.

**Vance → Full palette:** None of these colors appears in the top 20 mental health brand identities. The amber-on-parchment combination is ownable. Tide is held in reserve so professional differentiation is immediately visible.

**Wakabayashi → Warm bias:** Approach-motivation research associates warm hues with engagement and willingness to confront challenge. Cool hues activate avoidance circuits — the opposite of what Valid needs from its players.

### Approved Color Combinations

| Pairing | Use |
|---------|-----|
| Ground on Parchment | All body text. The primary reading experience. |
| Ember on Ground | Dark contexts, professional card face, section accents. |
| Ground on Ember | CTA elements only. Use sparingly. |
| Tide on Ground | Professional version differentiation. Never in Social version. |

## 04 — Typography

Two typefaces. Both chosen for what they are not: not the rounded approachability performance of Nunito or Poppins, and not the geometric coldness of clinical distance. This pairing signals that something was carefully considered — including the person reading it.

### Cormorant Garamond
*Display · Headings · Scenario text · Persona quotes*

Humanist details and high stroke contrast give Cormorant warmth without sentimentality. The italic is essential — it signals the voice of the scenario, not the voice of the game. Nobody in mental health branding is using this type. That is the point.

Weights: 400 Regular · 400 Italic · 500 Medium · 600 SemiBold · 600 Italic

### DM Sans
*Body copy · Labels · UI elements · Instructions*

DM Sans carries the operational weight — instructions, rules of play, response guidance, score labels. Clean without being cold. Precise without triggering clinical associations. It is not Inter — that distinction matters for this brand.

Weights: 400 Regular · 500 Medium

### Type Scale

| Level | Size | Typeface | Weight | Use |
|-------|------|----------|--------|-----|
| Display | 80–104px | Cormorant Garamond | 600 | Brand hero moments only |
| Heading 1 | 42–52px | Cormorant Garamond | 600 | Section titles |
| Heading 2 | 28–34px | Cormorant Garamond | 500 | Subsection titles |
| Body Large | 18px | DM Sans | 400 | Lead paragraphs, intros |
| Body | 16px | DM Sans | 400 | All running body text |
| Label | 10–11px | DM Sans | 500 | Tags, metadata, UI labels |
| Scenario | 20–24px | Cormorant Garamond | 400 italic | Card scenario text |

### Typography Rules

- Scenario text always in Cormorant Garamond italic — it is a voice, not a heading
- Labels always in small caps with 0.15–0.2em letter-spacing
- Never use Poppins, Nunito, Lato, or rounded sans-serifs anywhere in the system
- Never use Inter — it reads as startup-generic in this context
- Line-height 1.65 for body; 1.0–1.1 for large display settings
- Letter-spacing −0.02em on display sizes; 0 on body; +0.15em on labels

## 05 — Voice & Tone

Valid speaks the way a trusted expert does in a candid moment: without hedging, without cheerleading, with full confidence that you can handle what is true. The brand does not perform care. It exercises it.

### Contrast Pairs

**Avoid — Performance language:**  
"It's okay not to be okay. Valid gives you the tools to show up for the people you love, one compassionate conversation at a time. 💙"

*Why it fails:* Performs care rather than offering it. Borrows language already drained of meaning. The emoji signals "we are not taking this seriously." Could apply to any product marketed to any human.

**Use — Specific claim language:**  
"When someone you love is in crisis, your existing instincts were built for a different game. Valid teaches the responses that actually work — before the moment that demands them."

*Why it works:* Names a specific problem. Makes a specific claim. Trusts the reader to want the skill, not the feeling of having acquired it. The sentence can only be about Valid.

---

**Avoid — Reassurance language:**  
"No judgment here. This is a safe space to practice and grow. Everyone's journey looks different, and that's beautiful."

*Why it fails:* "Safe space" is a placeholder for the actual promise. "Beautiful" costs nothing and means less. This copy generates warmth for the writer, not trust for the reader.

**Use — Evidence language:**  
"Retrieval practice produces 50–100% better skill retention than passive reading. Valid uses that mechanism deliberately — not because it sounds good, but because it works."

*Why it works:* Cites evidence without condescension. Shows the work. This is the register of someone who actually read the literature before writing the copy. Clinicians will recognize it immediately.

---

**Avoid — Campaign language:**  
"Mental health awareness starts with YOU. Share Valid with someone who needs it today. Together we can break the stigma. 🌿"

*Why it fails:* "Break the stigma" is detached from any specific product action. Campaign language is borrowed territory — it belongs to awareness months, not products.

**Use — Grounded claim language:**  
"The relationship is the treatment infrastructure. Valid is built on research showing that relationships that survive crisis episodes intact predict significantly better long-term recovery outcomes."

*Why it works:* Specific, evidence-grounded, product-relevant. The clinical claim is also the emotional claim. This earns its weight rather than performing urgency borrowed from a broader movement.

### Voice Principles

**01 — Cite, don't claim.** Every bold statement is grounded in research Valid has actually read. Not because it is legally safer, but because the audience includes clinicians who will check — and the brand must hold up to that scrutiny.

**02 — Name the specific problem.** Generic mental health language floats above the actual difficulty. Valid always names the specific scenario, the specific gap, the specific skill being built. Specificity is a form of respect.

**03 — No borrowed urgency.** "Break the stigma." "You are not alone." "It's okay to ask for help." These phrases are empty from overuse. Valid earns its urgency through specificity, not through slogans whose weight belongs to someone else.

**04 — Warm, not soft.** Warmth is a posture, not a vocabulary. Valid speaks warmly by trusting the reader's intelligence — not by rounding the edges off difficult truths to protect the brand from the weight of the subject.

## 06 — Visual Language

Valid lives in the space between receiving a moment of crisis and knowing how to respond. The visual language embodies that space: card geometry, negative space as an active element, typography as primary image, and the deliberate absence of the wellness iconography that makes every other mental health brand invisible.

### Never Use

- Leaves, plants, or nature imagery of any kind
- Abstract human figures in embrace or proximity
- Puzzle pieces — especially the brain variant
- Gradient mesh, blob, or aurora backgrounds
- The color purple (mental health ribbon association)
- Stock photography of calm, soft-lit people
- Rounded corners as the default — signals approachability over precision
- Emoji in any brand-level touchpoint
- Borrowed phrases: "safe space," "you are not alone," "break the stigma"
- Drop shadows or glow effects on any element

### Always Use

- Typography as the primary visual object — especially Cormorant in scenario text
- Card geometry as a recurring structural motif across all touchpoints
- Negative space that creates weight, not decoration
- Thin rules (0.5–1px) as structural elements and section separators
- Ember as an accent — never used as a field color or large background
- Parchment / Ground as the only background-text pairing
- Small caps with tracked spacing for labels, version tags, metadata
- Tide exclusively for professional version differentiation
- Asymmetric type composition — the card face is never perfectly centered
- Sharp corners on card elements — precision over approachability

### The Primary Motif

The card face is Valid's visual anchor. Every design proportion, every typographic hierarchy decision, every use of negative space flows from the card. It is not decorative — it is the place where the user's thinking happens. The space between the scenario text and the instruction line at the bottom is where Valid actually lives.

### The Three Brand Moments

| Moment | Context | Voice |
|--------|---------|-------|
| Before play | Acquisition, packaging | "Your instincts were built for a different game." |
| The valid moment | Gameplay, card face | "That sounds terrifying. I'm right here." |
| After play | Follow-up, reflection | "The response that helps, before the moment demands it." |

## 07 — Application: The Card, Expressed

The brand expressed in its primary object. Note how the same visual system bifurcates cleanly at the card level: Social version (Ember / Parchment) and Professional version (Tide / Ground). The structure is identical — the context changes the signal entirely.

### Social Version Card — Real Talk
*Background: Parchment · Accent: Ember · Typeface: Cormorant + DM Sans*

```
REAL TALK — SOCIAL VERSION

Depression · Scenario 04 · Family Circle

"Your best friend texts at midnight:
'I don't see the point in trying anymore.'"

WRITE YOUR FIRST INSTINCT. THEN TURN THIS CARD.
```

### Professional Version Card — Therapeutic Connections
*Background: Ground · Accent: Tide · Typeface: Cormorant + DM Sans*

```
THERAPEUTIC CONNECTIONS — PROFESSIONAL

Suicidal Ideation · Intermediate · Therapist–Client

"A patient discloses passive suicidal ideation
during a scheduled medication review.
You have eight minutes remaining in the session."

FORMULATE YOUR CLINICAL RESPONSE.
CONSIDER: ASSESSMENT, ALLIANCE, TIME CONSTRAINT.
```

### Differentiation System

| Element | Social Version | Professional Version |
|---------|---------------|---------------------|
| Background | Parchment `#F2EDDF` | Ground `#1A1814` |
| Top accent rule | Ember `#C4882A` | Tide `#3D6B65` |
| Version tag color | Ember | Tide |
| Category label | Drift | Drift (reversed) |
| Scenario text | Cormorant 400 | Cormorant 400 |
| Instruction text | DM Sans small caps | DM Sans small caps |
| Corner mark | Ground 20% | Parchment 20% |

## Summary: What the Audit Produced

The four-persona method did not produce a compromise — it produced convergence. Each voice, from its distinct expertise, arrived at the same place through different reasoning:

Osei needed clinical credibility → warm, weighted type signals earned authority.

Bassi needed to be treated like an adult → no pastels, high contrast, no performance language.

Vance needed market differentiation → nothing in this palette or type system exists in the competitive set.

Wakabayashi needed approach-motivation alignment → warm hues + high contrast + clean information architecture.

The system that emerged is not what any one of them would have designed alone. It is what all four of them could not argue against.
